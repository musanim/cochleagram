Cochleagram

Sound, seen the way the ear resolves it.

Running here, in your browser. Allow the microphone and talk, whistle, or play something — or drop an audio file on the picture.

A cochleagram is a picture of sound over time, like a spectrogram — but drawn by a model of the inner ear rather than by a Fourier transform. Six hundred filters, sixty to the octave, tuned to the bandwidths human hearing actually uses.

An FFT-based spectrogram picks one trade-off between time and frequency and applies it everywhere: a long window separates low harmonics and smears every attack, a short one does the reverse. The ear resolves frequency finely at the bottom and time finely at the top, so both are visible in one picture.

Things worth trying

  1. Speed changes how much time is on screen. Moving fast, you can see voiced speech as a series of separate pulses rather than a texture.
  2. Pause, then drag across the picture: a pair of lines appears with the time between them. Hovering anywhere reads out the frequency of that row.
  3. Play Selection appears once you have paused the microphone. It plays the sound the frozen picture was drawn from — the whole width of it, or just what lies between your two lines. Hearing a moment you have just measured is the quickest way to learn to read the display.
  4. Waveform puts the sound itself in a strip above the picture, column for column, so you can see which part of the waveform made which mark.
  5. Spectrum stands the newest column on its side, to the right of the picture: loudness against frequency, at this instant, on the same rows. Drag the edge it shares with the picture to give it more room.
  6. ERB is how sharp the filters are. 1.0 matches human hearing; lower is sharper than the ear and easier to read.
  7. Close-up gives the last fraction of a second its own strip down the right-hand side, at much finer resolution, flowing into the main picture. Drag the line down its left-hand side to change how much room it gets.
  8. De-skew straightens a click. Sound takes time to travel down the cochlea, so a click really does reach the low filters later — de-skew compensates, at the cost of lag: about 190 ms at the default ERB, and more the sharper you make the filters.

The Mac app

The same engine, natively. It has one thing the browser version does not: an experimental Coherence display that colours the picture by the timing relationship between neighbouring filters rather than by loudness — red for transient, green for tone, blue for noise. It is an experiment rather than a finished feature, which is why it has not been carried across.
